Living on the I Chord

The ii and the V are places you pass through. Cmaj7 is where the tune actually spends its time, and it is the one chord you cannot fix by altering — bend the wrong note and it stops being home. So the answers here are different in kind: extend it, rename it, put a melody on top of it, or fill the bar with something else entirely. Four parts, each answering a different question.

What else can I put in that bar?

Substitutions

Twelve chords that can stand where Cmaj7 stands, ordered by how far each one travels from it. Same format as the Dm7 and G7 sheets: a compact comping grip, and a full grip with the root in the bass.

How do I get a melody out of it?

Chord melody

Stop thinking of the grip as a block and start thinking of the top string as a singer. The chord underneath barely changes; the note on string 1 is the whole performance. Two ways in — hold one shape and walk the top finger, or work backwards from the note you want to hear.

One shape, four chords

Fret x x 5 5 5 – and leave it there. The only thing that moves is your little finger on string 1, walking down C – B – B♭ – A. The harmony changes underneath it by itself: tonic, major seven, dominant, sixth. This is the oldest device on the instrument and still the most useful — it turns one bar of Cmaj7 into a line that goes somewhere.

Play it in time and the ear hears a descending melody, not four chords. Run it backwards for a rising line, or stop on the C7 and go to Fmaj7 — you have just written a modulation.

Pick the melody note first

The reverse method, and the one that actually makes chord melody workable on a tune: decide what note the melody needs, then reach for the grip that puts that note on top. Every one of these is a Cmaj7 family chord — what changes is which degree is singing.

Notice the pattern in the degrees: the low grips put R and 5 on top and sound settled; the high ones put 9 and ♯11 up there and sound like they are still thinking. That is the whole expressive range of a I chord, and it is controlled by one finger.

What do I do with the rest of the time?

Turnarounds and vamps

How long you are stuck on the I decides the strategy. A bar or two, and the answer is a turnaround — motion that leads back into the ii. Four bars or more, and turnarounds sound frantic; you want devices that stay still and change colour instead.

Short form · one or two bars

Six standard turnarounds, in rough order of how far out they go. Every one of them starts on the tonic bar and hands you back to Dm7 or straight to C. Read them as one bar per cell — halve that and you have a two-beat turnaround.

The rule underneath all six: any dominant can be swapped for the one a tritone away, and any chord can be preceded by its own ii. Those two moves generate every turnaround anyone plays.

Long form · four bars or more

When the harmony will not move, move the upper structure over it. Hold C in the bass — open string, a pedal from the bass player, or just the memory of one — and play these triads above it. They are listed by brightness: the further down the list, the further from the ground.

C is home and Em is Cmaj7 exactly. G adds the 9th, Am swaps the 7th for the 6th, and the last two are Lydian — Bm and D both carry the F♯ and neither one contains a note that wants to resolve. Move between them slowly over a static bass and four bars go by without anyone noticing the chord never changed.

What do I solo with?

Lines over Cmaj7

C major over Cmaj7 is technically correct and almost always dull, because one note in it — the F — is a half step above the third and lands like a mistake wherever you leave it. Every good option below is a way of dodging that note.

Three pentatonics, one fifth apart

The same five-note shape rooted on C, G and D. Each step up the circle brightens the sound and moves the box up two frets, and not one of the fifteen notes involved is an F. Degrees are shown against C, not against the pentatonic's own root — that is the whole point.

Arpeggios you can superimpose

Same logic as the triads in Part Three, played one note at a time. Em7 arpeggiated over C gives you 3–5–7–9. Gmaj7 gives 5–7–9–♯11 and pulls the whole thing Lydian. Am7 gives 13–R–3–5 and sounds like C6. Start a line on any of the three and the ear reads it as Cmaj7 with an opinion, not as the wrong chord.

If you want the F back — and sometimes you do — treat it as a passing note on a weak beat and keep moving. The problem was never the note; it was stopping on it.

The short version

If you take one thing from this sheet: the I chord does not get more interesting by being replaced. It gets more interesting by moving. A single top voice walking down four frets does more than any substitution here, and it works on every tune in the book.

The substitutions are for when the melody has stopped and the space is yours. The chord melody is for when the melody is the point. The turnarounds are for when neither is true and the bar still has to go somewhere.
