Getting Out of ii–V–I

Reference sheets for the progression every standard is built from. Three of them take a single chord — Dm7, G7, Cmaj7 — and lay out what you can play instead of it, as guitar chord diagrams with the scale degrees printed inside the dots. The fourth puts the whole library to work on one twelve-bar vamp, arranged three ways.

Dm7ii G7V Cmaj7I 3 Arrangementsexamples

Why the key of C. Everything here transposes by sliding. No open strings are load-bearing in any grip, so the whole library moves up or down the neck as one piece — C to B♭ is the same shapes two frets lower.

Why scale degrees, not fingerings. A dot marked ♭7 tells you what the note is doing. Once you can see that the same grip is a G7♯9 or a D♭13 depending only on the bass note, the substitution stops being a rule to memorise.

Two grips for everything. A compact comping voicing for when a bass player has the root covered, and a full grip with the root in the bass for playing alone. Same chord, different job.

Twelve Ways Out of Dm7

The ii chord is a place you pass through, so most of what works here is addition rather than alteration — stack thirds on top of D and every stop is still Dm7 with a taller extension. Past that: suspend it, borrow it from C minor, turn it into a dominant, or move the root a tritone away.

The payoff is seeing that A♭13 and D7♯9 are the same grip, told apart only by the bass note.

Twelve Ways Out of G7

The V is the one chord in the progression where almost anything is legal — it is leaving anyway. Extend it, suspend the third, alter every note that is not a guide tone, move the root a tritone, or skip it entirely and reach the tonic through the back door.

B and F are the two notes that make it a G7. Everything else on the sheet is negotiable.

sheet three · the library

Living on the I Chord

The tonic is where a tune actually spends its time, and it is the one chord you cannot fix by altering — bend the wrong note and it stops being home. So this sheet is built in four parts, each answering a different question: what else can go in the bar, how to get a melody out of it, what to do with the rest of the time, and what to solo with.

The one to steal first: hold x x 5 5 5 – and walk your little finger down string 1. The chord changes underneath you by itself.

How to use these

Read a sheet top to bottom once, then stop. Take one substitution into a tune you already play and use only that one for a week. The sheets are built as reference, not as a curriculum — the ordering by distance from the original chord is there so you can tell how far you are stepping out before you step.

Every grip is checked note by note against the fretboard: each printed scale degree matches the pitch that string actually sounds, and no voicing spans more than four frets.

Sheet four is the other way round — it is not a library to browse but a single vamp played through three times, each pass changing one thing. If the first three sheets leave you with more options than ideas, start there instead.
